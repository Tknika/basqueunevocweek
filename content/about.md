Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) systems worldwide face the same broad challenges: inclusion, sustainability, digitalization, demographic change, the TVET-business relationship, innovation, the threats to democracy, and the need to keep pace with evolving labour markets. Named at this level of generality, the agenda looks remarkably similar from one country to the next.

Yet these shared categories take very different shapes once they meet a specific place. Inclusion means everywhere that no learner is left behind, but who is at risk of being left behind differs from place to place: young people who leave school early in one, women in industrial trades in another, adults in rural areas or newly arrived workers in a third. Digitalization means one thing in a system building its first data infrastructure and another in a system already integrating AI into daily teaching practice. Sustainability means one thing for an economy built on manufacturing and another for one built on tourism or agriculture. The categories are global; the solutions being built to address them are irreducibly local, shaped by each region, economic structure, institutions, and history.

This is where TVET matters most. TVET is the most local of education systems: it is delivered in schools and training centres rooted in a territory, it is built around the skills needs of the companies and communities around it, and it is where a young person or an adult finds a path to a qualification and a job in the place where they live. Global shifts (a new technology, the green transition, an ageing workforce) only become real when they reach a local company that adopts them, a local school that adapts its curriculum, and a local trainer who learns to teach them. Global agendas such as the Sustainable Development Goals or the digital and green transitions are, in practice, delivered or not through local TVET ecosystems. The relevance of TVET at local level is therefore not a side issue: it is the point at which global challenges are turned into opportunities for people.

This is why international cooperation matters, and why it has to work in both directions. From the global to the local: shared international frameworks, evidence and benchmarks give each local ecosystem a wider view than its own, and a way to test its choices against what others are learning. From the local to the global: a local ecosystem that solves a version of the digitalization challenge, or designs a working model for entrepreneurship education, or finds an effective way to close a gender gap in technical training, has produced something of value to other ecosystems facing a differently-shaped version of the same problem. Implementation will always be local — no model transplants without adaptation — but the learning does not have to be. Connecting the people who are doing this work, so that they can see what others are building and adapt it to their own context, is itself a form of impact. It is what allows local ecosystems to keep learning from one another, long after any single event. It is with this in mind that one of the central objectives of this gathering is to surface concrete local solutions and practices that could inform or inspire adaptation in other contexts, reinforcing the idea that while implementation is always local, learning does not have to be.

In recent years there has been a visible pull toward national or regional self-sufficiency, toward solving problems in isolation rather than in dialogue with peers elsewhere. We think this is the wrong direction for TVET. Because TVET draws its strength from local economies and communities, a system that turns inward loses the very thing that keeps it relevant locally: fresh ideas, evidence and partners from elsewhere. The challenges are too complex, and too widely shared in their broad outlines, for any single system to work through them alone. This gathering is built on the conviction that global connection, and the deliberate sharing of knowledge, practice, and experience across contexts, is not a nicety but a necessity. Networks like UNEVOC exist precisely to make that sharing possible at scale. Accordingly, the event also aims to identify concrete opportunities for future collaboration, joint projects, and knowledge exchange between participating institutions, as a deliberate counterweight to recent tendencies toward working in isolation.

Against this backdrop, the timing of this gathering carries particular significance. 2026 marks 25 years since the establishment of the UNESCO-UNEVOC International Centre, a milestone for a network that has spent a quarter of a century doing exactly this kind of connective work. In the same year, Tknika marks 10 years as a UNEVOC centre. This coincidence gives the gathering, jointly organised by the Department of Education of the Basque Government and UNESCO-UNEVOC, a third guiding purpose: to mark, jointly, the 25th anniversary of UNESCO-UNEVOC and the 10th anniversary of Tknika’s membership in the UNEVOC Network, recognising both the achievements of the global network and the contribution of one of its long-standing regional centres.

The conference and its accompanying week are therefore conceived not only as a forward-looking exercise in strategic exchange, but as a moment to celebrate this joint anniversary: a network reaching a quarter-century of global cooperation, and one of its regional centres marking ten years of active, two-way contribution to that network.

Anyone interested in sharing and learning how TVET systems worldwide are tackling these key challenges should not miss this gathering. **It is especially relevant for:**

- TVET school representatives
- Policymakers, authorities, and government representatives
- International organisations active in the fields of TVET and labour
- Social partners
- Employers
- Researchers and practitioners in TVET innovation, entrepreneurship education, digital transformation, and green skills

To serve these purposes for the different audiences the gathering brings together, the programme offers distinct options rather than a single one-size-fits-all agenda. Policymakers are offered a policy dialogue, study visits, and the conference itself. TVET students are offered two separate one-week training programmes, one at EQF levels 3–4 (ISCED 3) and one at EQF levels 5–6 (ISCED 5), giving each group a learning experience matched to their stage of study. TVET representatives, employers, and researchers are offered dedicated workshops and study visit programmes suited to their practical and professional interests. This differentiated design reflects the same principle underlying the rationale above: that shared challenges are best addressed when the format of engagement is adapted to who is in the room, even as everyone works toward the same underlying goals of mutual learning and renewed collaboration.
